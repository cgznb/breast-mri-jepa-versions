Abstract
Longitudinal breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers repeated mea-
surements of treatment response, but discriminative models do not necessarily
exploit the future disease trajectories implied by an observed examination.
We investigate whether auxiliary future-state prediction and an explicit
forecast-based readout improve prediction of pathological complete response
(pCR). The framework combines three-phase dynamic contrast-enhanced
MRI, baseline clinical variables, and assigned treatment-arm scenarios in
a latent representation. An autoregressive predictor estimates unobserved
future states, and an attention readout summarizes the predicted terminal
state and its change from the current state. We study 976 patients with 3,641
usable MRI examinations, using 801 patients for five-fold development and a
fixed internal holdout of 175 patients, including 56 pCR-positive cases. Three
matched variants isolate direct prediction without future-state supervision,
direct prediction with that supervision, and the complete forecast-attention
model. This initial analysis reports training seed44, selected retrospectively,
and averages metrics from separately evaluated fold models. At T0, T1, and
T2, the complete model achieved AUROCs of 0.7294, 0.7381, and 0.7421,
respectively. Its three-landmark mean AUROC and average precision were
0.7365 and 0.5990, compared with 0.7219 and 0.5792 for the control without
future-state supervision. Direct readout retained the highest mean F1, and
forecast attention had worse calibration. Paired patient-bootstrap intervals
included zero for the principal model differences. These exploratory findings
support further evaluation of forecast-aware representations while motivating
independent validation, prospective model selection, and explicit calibration
assessment.

1. Introduction
    Neoadjuvant systemic therapy provides an opportunity to monitor breast
cancer before surgery and to study how an individual tumor responds over time.
Pathological complete response (pCR) is a clinically meaningful endpoint
associated with long-term outcomes, although its relationship to prognosis
varies across biological subtypes and it should not be equated with a universally
valid trial-level surrogate (Cortazar et al., 2014). Predicting pCR from imaging
could support response assessment and the design of future adaptive studies.
Such a prediction remains a prognostic estimate under an observed treatment
context; it does not establish that changing treatment would improve an
individual patient’s outcome.
    Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI captures three-dimensional anatomy
and contrast uptake patterns that evolve during therapy. Prior work in the
I-SPY setting has demonstrated associations between MRI-derived tumor
measurements and pathological response or subsequent outcomes (Hylton
et al., 2012, 2016). Public multicenter imaging resources also make it possible
to examine learned representations across repeated visits (Li et al., 2022;
Newitt et al., 2021). However, a longitudinal prediction system must operate
at the time a decision is considered: a baseline prediction can use baseline
information, whereas an interim prediction can use the examination history
accumulated by that interim visit. Future scans may inform representation
learning on development patients but cannot become inputs to predictions
for holdout patients at an earlier landmark.
    Most direct classifiers optimize the endpoint of interest from observed
images and clinical variables. This is a reasonable objective, but it does not
explicitly require the representation to capture how the tumor state may
evolve. A different strategy is to predict future imaging information and use
the prediction to inform the endpoint readout. Image synthesis provides one
route to such a representation, but prediction in a compact latent space can
focus the training signal on state evolution without requiring a pixel-space
image generator at inference. This distinction is useful for response prediction:
the relevant question is whether the learned forecast contributes information
to classification, rather than whether a generated image is visually convincing.

    Joint-embedding predictive learning offers a general mechanism for pre-
dicting target representations computed by a slowly updated encoder (Assran
et al., 2023). In a longitudinal medical setting, adapting that principle requires
explicit handling of partial histories, missing future observations, treatment
context, and the distinction between an auxiliary training target and a classi-
fier input. A model may benefit from a future-state loss while still making a
direct prediction from the observed history. Conversely, directly including
forecast features in the classifier changes both its information pathway and
its parameterization. These effects should be examined through matched
controls rather than attributed to a single generic “world model” component.
    We investigate forecast-aware latent modeling for longitudinal pCR pre-
diction from breast MRI. The model encodes three selected DCE phases at
each available visit, fuses observed MRI with baseline clinical information
and an assigned treatment-arm scenario, and autonomously rolls the current
latent state forward to a terminal stage. A spatial attention mechanism
summarizes the predicted terminal state and the predicted change from the
current state for pCR classification. We compare the complete model with
two direct-readout controls: one without future-state supervision and one
with the same auxiliary supervision as the complete model. A supplementary
mean-pooling model further examines the attention component.
    The study makes three concrete contributions. First, it formulates a
common representation and masked future-state objective that can be ap-
plied to available T0–T2 histories while using observed later scans only as
development-time targets. Second, it provides a staged comparison that
distinguishes the effect of auxiliary prediction from the joint effect of forecast
readout and attention. Third, it reports landmark-specific discrimination,
thresholded classification, calibration, and paired patient-level uncertainty on
a fixed internal holdout. The initial manuscript follows a single, retrospec-
tively selected training seed, seed44. Accordingly, the results are presented
as an exploratory internal evaluation, with the limits of seed selection and
upstream data independence stated explicitly.

2. Related work
MRI biomarkers and learned pCR prediction.. MRI-based response assessment
has a substantial clinical and quantitative foundation. Functional tumor
volume and related measurements have been studied in longitudinal breast
MRI cohorts, including I-SPY (Hylton et al., 2012, 2016). The ACRIN 6698

                                        3
study additionally examined diffusion-derived measurements in relation to
response (Partridge et al., 2018). These studies motivate imaging-based
monitoring but do not establish that a learned model using different inputs or
a different evaluation split has equivalent clinical validity. Our experiments
use selected DCE phases and baseline clinical variables; diffusion imaging is
not an input to the present model.
    Learning-based approaches can combine complementary image sequences
or repeated examinations. Synthesis-based representation learning has been
explored for multi-sequence 3D/4D MRI (Han et al., 2024), and recent
studies have examined spatiotemporal or early-treatment information for
pCR prediction (Tang et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025). Such studies provide
relevant methodological context, but differences in patient selection, imaging
availability, endpoints, and holdout construction prevent direct numerical
comparisons with our results. We therefore do not claim superiority to
published systems from cross-paper metric comparisons. Reimplementation
of suitable external baselines under the same patient split remains necessary.

Predictive latent representations.. Predicting a target embedding rather than
reconstructing the input image is a central idea in joint-embedding predictive
learning (Assran et al., 2023). We use an exponential-moving-average target
encoder to define latent states and supervise autonomous future predictions.
The present objective is a supervised pCR loss combined with a masked multi-
step latent-state loss. It does not activate a separate single-step teacher-forcing
JEPA loss, image reconstruction loss, or latent-variance regularizer. This
objective-level distinction matters when interpreting the internal experiment
name NoJEPA: that control removes future-state supervision, while retaining
the common fusion architecture.

Attention and volumetric image representation.. Transformer attention pro-
vides a flexible way to combine image tokens and contextual information
(Vaswani et al., 2017). Compact convolutional representations can reduce the
cost of processing volumetric MRI, and vector-quantized generative models
provide one source of pretrained convolutional encoders (Esser et al., 2021).
Here the pretrained codec contributes a continuous convolutional encoder.
The quantizer and image decoder are bypassed, and the main experiments do
not synthesize MRI volumes. The final attention weights summarize latent
tokens that have already undergone spatial mixing; they are therefore not
validated lesion segmentations or anatomical explanations.

3.2. Continuous MRI encoding and multimodal fusion
    Each observed examination is encoded independently by an online visit
encoder Eθ . Its convolutional front end shares the same single-channel
weights across the three acquisition phases. Two stride-two three-dimensional
convolutional stages, residual blocks, and group normalization produce 64
continuous feature channels per phase at one-quarter input resolution along
each spatial axis. The three phase-specific feature maps are concatenated,
adaptively averaged to a 2 × 4 × 4 grid, and projected to a latent width of
d = 768. This gives N = 32 spatial tokens per examination. Learned spatial
positional embeddings and an eight-layer spatial Transformer then produce
the visit representation Zt = Eθ (Xt ) ∈ RN ×d .
    The convolutional front end is initialized from the same pre-existing VQ-
GAN-style codec checkpoint for all compared models and is fine-tuned. Here,
the model uses continuous features from the codec encoder before its compres-
sion projection. It does not use discrete codebook assignments, a quantization
objective, a decoder, or adversarial learning during downstream pCR train-
ing. [TO COMPLETE: Document the original codec pretraining dataset,

Encoding a future target in this branch supplies a loss target only; it does

    The predictor starts from the latest actually observed state in Hk and
recursively advances to T3. For the reported landmarks, this initial state is
Sk . At each future transition, it uses the corresponding transition features
and its previously generated states. A history input additionally embeds the
difference between a source state and its latest available predecessor, with
a separate indicator for whether a predecessor exists. Observed history is

4. Dataset and preprocessing
4.1. Cohort construction and clinical variables

enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) from the public I-SPY2 and ACRIN-6698

imaging identifiers belonged to the ISPY2 and ACRIN-6698 namespaces for
712 and 264 patients, respectively; these identifiers were mapped to the same
clinical release and do not define independent development and external
validation cohorts. The official collection pages identify the source imaging
and clinical release as CC BY 4.0. [TO COMPLETE: Confirm terms for

4.2. Patient partitioning and evaluation scope
    The cohort was divided into 801 development patients and a fixed internal
holdout of 175 patients, including 56 pCR-positive patients. All holdout
patients had actual T0, T1, and T2 examinations, allowing matched longitu-
dinal evaluation; 163 also had T3. The holdout retained 139 patients with
complete T0–T2 observations from the historical benchmark and added 36
eligible patients. Additional patients were selected using outcome quotas
and a fixed baseline-clinical distribution balancing rule with split seed 2026,
without using model predictions or performance. Development folds were
stratified by pCR and examination-availability pattern; rare joint strata were
combined within the same pCR class. Each development patient appeared in
validation exactly once across the five folds. Fold 1 contained 640 training


                                           12
and 161 validation patients; the remaining folds each contained 641 training
and 160 validation patients. Training, validation, and holdout identities were
disjoint within each fold, and the same holdout was used for all five trained
models.
    This partition is an internal downstream benchmark. In particular, 29 of
the 36 added holdout patients had belonged to the development population of
an earlier experiment, so earlier trained models cannot be treated as held out
on the entire new set. The present downstream models were trained using
the new fold definitions. Patient independence of the supplied codec and
segmentation pretraining checkpoints relative to the final holdout has not yet
been established; that provenance must be audited before stronger claims of
end-to-end independence. The complete-examination holdout eligibility also
restricts inference about patients with missing early follow-up examinations.

4.3. Registration, fixed localisation, and normalisation
    Within each examination, postcontrast phases were registered to the
precontrast reference. Follow-up examinations were aligned to the patient’s
T0 reference using the saved longitudinal transforms, retaining a rigid fallback
when the final deformable candidate was not usable. Baseline localisation used
the existing MAMA-MIA nnU-Net prediction on the native first-postcontrast
examination Garrucho et al. (2025). The centre of the largest 26-connected
predicted component defined a fixed baseline crop, which was reused at every
actual follow-up. Follow-up segmentation and follow-up-driven recentering
were not used.
    Each examination was represented by three channels: the precontrast
phase, the first postcontrast phase, and the late phase explicitly specified in
the examination metadata. The late channel was not selected by assuming a
fixed phase number or taking the last available phase. Images were resampled
to 32 × 128 × 128 voxels with spacing 2.0 × 0.7032 × 0.7032 mm in the slice
and two in-plane dimensions, approximately covering 64 × 90 × 90 mm. Linear
interpolation was used for MRI intensities and nearest-neighbour interpolation
for masks and acquisition support, with zero padding outside the acquired
field of view. The localisation mask determined the crop; it was not a
contemporaneous ground-truth tumour segmentation or a future observation
supplied to the classifier.
    Native metadata, baseline mask headers, registered reference grids, and
saved transforms were maintained as matched versions. Explicit array-index
mappings, rather than placeholder NIfTI affines, defined sampling positions.

                                      13
The exported arrays were checked for finite values, consistent coordinate
mapping, and numerical agreement after compressed storage and readback.
These checks establish data and geometry consistency but do not establish
anatomical registration or segmentation accuracy. The fixed crop partly
truncated the largest predicted baseline component for 85 patients; these
patients were retained and the limitation was recorded.
    Raw cropped intensities were stored once and shared across folds. For each
fold, a single intensity mean and population standard deviation were fitted to
nonzero precontrast foreground voxels from every unique actual examination
of that fold’s training patients. The same scalar transform was applied once
to the foreground of all three channels, stages, and partitions; background and
padding remained zero. Validation and holdout images were excluded from
fitting. Age normalisation was likewise fitted to observed training-patient
values; HR, HER2, and MammaPrint retained their official binary coding,
with missingness represented explicitly.

5. Experimental design
5.1. Development protocol and landmark evaluation
    The 801 development patients were partitioned into five patient-level folds.
Fold 1 used 640 training and 161 validation patients; folds 2–5 each used
641 training and 160 validation patients. The same 175-patient holdout was
evaluated by every fold model and every variant. Thus the five test evaluations
per variant are repeated evaluations of overlapping trained models on the
same patients, rather than five independent test cohorts. Patient identities
were disjoint between training, validation, and holdout within the current
split.
    At each training epoch, a patient contributed one randomly selected
available T0, T1, or T2 prefix. Only MRI observations available at or before
the selected landmark were classifier inputs. Later observed visits, including
T3, could supply latent targets for the auxiliary loss on training patients.
Missing targets were masked. The 23 patients with only T0 remained eligible
for pCR training but supplied no future-state loss. At evaluation, all three
landmarks were evaluated for the 175 holdout patients, each of whom had
T0, T1, and T2. T3 was not an evaluation landmark and was not used as
observed input for these predictions.



                                      14
5.2. Model comparisons
    The principal comparison comprises three variants trained with seed44 and
the same five development folds. NoJEPA uses direct pCR readout and a
zero future-state loss weight. DirectReadout uses the same direct readout
with a future-state loss weight of 0.5. ForecastAttention retains that
auxiliary weight and replaces the direct readout with a concatenation of direct
pCR features, attention-pooled predicted terminal state, and attention-pooled
predicted change. All variants retain the shared image encoder, clinical inputs,
assigned-arm encoding, fusion architecture, and training schedule.
    The DirectReadout–NoJEPA contrast most directly tests adding
future-state supervision. The ForecastAttention–DirectReadout
contrast jointly changes the use of forecast features, spatial pooling, and
classifier input width. It cannot isolate attention alone. For that purpose, the
supplementary ForecastMean comparison uses forecast readout with mean
spatial pooling and otherwise matches the forecast-attention configuration. All
reported models in this draft use seed44. No VQ-latent reconstruction-head,
change-weighted-loss, or state-conditioned-head variants are included.

5.3. Implementation and optimization
    The network uses a model width of 768, eight layers in each of the spatial
encoder, multimodal fusion module, and predictor, 16 attention heads with 64
dimensions per head, and feed-forward width 3,072. The 1,024-dimensional
concatenated attention-head representation is projected back to the 768-
dimensional residual stream. The spatial token grid is 2 × 4 × 4. The
configuration uses eight state queries, four pCR queries, dropout 0.3, and an
EMA coefficient of 0.999. The pretrained convolutional encoder is fine-tuned
jointly with the remaining network. Its training-patient provenance requires
additional documentation, as discussed in Section 7.4.
    We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with an initial base learning
rate of 10−4 for both the encoder and the main network, weight decay 0.05,
batch size 16, no gradient accumulation, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0.
Training uses BF16 mixed precision. A ten-epoch warm-up increases the
learning rate from 10−5 to 10−4 , followed by cosine decay to 10−6 at epoch
100. Training is capped at 200 epochs, with early stopping after 50 epochs
without improvement. The best checkpoint is selected independently in each
fold using validation patient-equal-prefix F1 at threshold 0.5. This is an F1
selection protocol, even though AUROC is a principal reporting measure.

Deterministic execution was not enabled, so a fixed seed does not imply
bitwise reproducibility across hardware or software changes.

5.5. Scope of the selected-seed analysis
    The choice of seed44 for this manuscript was made retrospectively after
results from multiple training seeds were available. It was not a prospectively
registered seed or a selection made without knowledge of the holdout results.
Reporting the same seed for all models preserves a matched comparison within
that seed, but it does not remove selection bias or establish robustness across
training randomness. The analysis therefore evaluates an exploratory model
configuration rather than providing a confirmatory performance estimate.

7. Discussion
7.1. What the matched comparisons establish
    The seed44 analysis shows a descriptive increase in mean AUROC and AP
as the model progresses from direct prediction without future-state supervision
to auxiliary-supervised direct prediction and then to forecast-attention readout.

                                        19
The first contrast is consistent with future-state supervision encouraging an
observed-history representation that is useful for pCR prediction. The second
is consistent with additional predictive value in a classifier that receives
the forecast state and its change. However, the latter comparison also
changes classifier width and pooling, so it cannot attribute the difference
exclusively to a more accurate latent forecast. The supplementary mean-
pooling control narrows the pooling question but exhibits only a small mean
AUROC difference.
    This distinction also changes how the model should be described. The
main experiments do not reconstruct future MRI for classification and do
not establish that an imagined image reproduces a patient’s future anatomy.
Rather, an auxiliary latent objective regularizes a representation, and the full
classifier reads a predicted embedding. Moreover, the direct control named
NoJEPA retains the common fusion backbone and its transition queries. It
is an ablation of future-state supervision, not proof that every world-model-
related architectural component has been removed. Describing the actual
information paths is more informative than relying on the experiment labels
alone.

7.2. Longitudinal prediction and the role of treatment context
    The full model’s AUROC increased across T0–T2 in the selected seed,
and the larger differences relative to the direct control occurred at later
landmarks. One plausible explanation is that an observed response trajectory
helps condition later-state forecasts. Another is that the additional forecast
features or classifier capacity suit the later-prefix distribution. The current
experiments do not distinguish these explanations. Forecast-quality analysis,
frozen-forecast controls, parameter-matched direct classifiers, and prospective
landmark-specific validation would help determine the mechanism.
    Treatment is represented as an assigned-arm scenario assumed known at
baseline. In the source protocol, T0 MRI preceded randomization; therefore,
using the eventual assignment with T0 MRI is a retrospective conditional
analysis and cannot be interpreted as a deployable prediction before allocation.
The input does not encode the patient’s verified delivered dose, dose reductions,
actual cycle timing, adherence, or later treatment changes. Consequently, the
framework should be interpreted as response prediction conditional on an
encoded assignment, not as a simulator for treatment intervention. Comparing
predictions under different assigned-arm tokens would not estimate causal


                                       20
treatment effects without additional assumptions, appropriate treatment data,
and a separate causal analysis.

7.3. Discrimination is not calibrated clinical utility
    The forecast-attention model’s improved AUROC and AP coexisted with
lower mean F1 than auxiliary-supervised direct readout and worse NLL, Brier
score, and ECE than the control without future-state supervision. These
discrepancies are expected to matter if probabilities are used to support
decisions. AUROC measures ranking over thresholds, while F1 at 0.5 depends
on a particular operating point; neither alone certifies useful clinical risk
estimates. The model was selected by validation F1, not by a prespecified
utility function. Independent assessment of calibration, clinically motivated
operating points, and decision consequences is needed before suggesting a role
in patient management. The attention maps also require separate validation
before being interpreted as anatomical explanations.

7.4. Limitations and requirements for a confirmatory study
    The most immediate limitation is retrospective seed selection. Seed44 was
chosen for manuscript presentation after multiple-seed results were available,
including holdout results. A matched seed does not make this selection
prospective. Patient bootstrapping quantifies variation in the evaluated
patient sample conditional on the trained models, but cannot account for
selection among seeds or the randomness of retraining. All reported model
comparisons and intervals should therefore be considered exploratory. A
confirmatory study should lock the model, seed policy, endpoints, and analysis
before evaluating new patients.
    The second limitation concerns the evaluation cohort and upstream in-
dependence. The holdout is an internal downstream split, not an external
institution or independent prospective cohort. Twenty-nine of its patients had
belonged to a development split in an earlier experimental lineage. Current
supervised training uses the new patient-disjoint folds and does not warm-start
from those earlier pCR models. Nevertheless, the patient-level overlap of the
pretrained codec and localization-segmentation development data with the
current holdout has not been fully established. This unresolved provenance
prevents a claim that the complete pipeline has been assessed on entirely
unseen patients at every stage.
    Third, the fixed crop and preprocessing introduce constraints. A T0-
derived localization window is used for all visits, and some predicted lesion

                                      21
components extend beyond the fixed field of view. Geometry and array-
consistency checks do not substitute for anatomical review of segmentation
or registration. The spatial window may underrepresent multifocal disease,
chest-wall extent, or changes outside the crop. Complete T0–T2 availability
is also required for holdout inclusion, whereas the development set retains
incomplete histories. Performance may therefore differ for patients with
missing interim MRI, and the missingness process may itself be associated
with clinical factors.
    Fourth, the present empirical comparison comprises matched internal
variants. It does not yet establish performance against clinical-only models,
conventional imaging biomarkers, independent longitudinal architectures, or
contemporary published methods under the same split. Patient-subgroup
analysis, scanner and site stratification, subtype-specific sensitivity, and the
relationship between forecast error and classification error are also incomplete.
The relatively small holdout, with 56 positive outcomes, limits the precision
of those analyses and the principal paired estimates.
    Finally, several reporting details still require confirmation from original
clinical documentation: the exact pCR pathology definition and assessment
workflow, actual patient-level acquisition intervals and treatment exposure,
clinical-data adjudication, and the applicable ethics determination. The
nominal stage timing is documented, but adherence and interval distributions
have not yet been audited. These are explicit outstanding items in this draft
rather than assumptions filled from common practice. Independent external
validation, complete pretraining provenance, suitable external baselines, and
a prespecified analysis would materially strengthen the evidence for a final
Medical Image Analysis submission.

8. Conclusion
    We investigated a latent forecasting framework for longitudinal pCR
prediction from breast MRI using matched direct and forecast-based controls.
In the retrospectively selected seed44 analysis, forecast-attention readout
produced the highest mean AUROC and AP among the principal variants,
while auxiliary-supervised direct readout retained the highest mean F1. The
complete model’s calibration was worse, and paired patient-bootstrap intervals
included zero for the principal differences. The findings motivate further study
of future-state supervision and forecast-based readout, with prospective model

validation required before a confirmatory or clinical-use claim.
